120 Volt Tankless Water Heater: What It Can Really Do In Lehi

A 120 volt tankless heater is a point of use tool, not a whole house answer. Here is where it fits and where it falls short.

By the Lehi Pro Plumbing team · Published September 15, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(385) 488-1892
☎ Call Now

A 120 volt tankless water heater can heat water for a single sink, a small hand wash station or a short shower at a very low flow. It cannot heat a whole house. The power it can draw from a standard household outlet is a small fraction of what a whole house tankless unit needs.

If you want one small tap to run hot without waiting, a plug in unit is a fair choice. If you want showers and laundry from the same heater, you need a gas unit or a high amperage electric unit. Our tankless water heater page covers those options.

The Physics

Why Power Limits The Output

Heating water takes energy. A shower at a modest flow can need a lot of power, because the heater must raise the temperature of cold incoming water by tens of degrees every second. A 120 volt outlet on a typical household circuit cannot supply that much power safely.

Whole house electric tankless units use large dedicated circuits at higher voltage. Gas units use burners rated in thousands of BTU per hour. Both are installed with a plan, and that plan often includes a panel check or a gas line upgrade.

120 volt plug in unit

  • Good for one sink or a hand wash station
  • Plugs into a standard outlet
  • Lower flow, limited temperature rise
  • Not meant for showers or laundry

Whole house tankless

  • Feeds several fixtures at once
  • Needs a gas line or a high amperage circuit
  • Needs venting if gas
  • Sized by flow rate and winter water temperature
Good Uses

Places A Small Unit Makes Sense

Distant bathroom sink

A tap far from the water heater can take a long time to run hot. A small unit at the sink fixes the wait.

Garage or shop sink

Hot water for washing hands and parts without running a line from the house heater.

Cabin, ADU or guest suite

A single sink or small kitchenette that does not justify a full water heater.

Kitchen prep sink

A small booster for rinsing when you do not want to wait for the main heater.

Even in these cases, check the circuit. Some small units use more power than a shared outlet circuit can safely carry.

Before You Shop

What Needs A Plan Before You Buy

Questions to answer first

  • How many gallons per minute do you need at the fixture?
  • How cold is the incoming water in January?
  • Is there a dedicated circuit with enough capacity?
  • Does the manufacturer require a certain water pressure or flow?
  • Who will handle the plumbing connections and any permit?

Keeping It Working

Maintenance And Scale

Small tankless units have small heat exchangers, and scale can build up inside them. If you notice white crust on your fixtures, test your tap before you buy any heater. A test kit costs little and tells you whether a flush plan or a softener belongs in the picture. See our softener and filtration page.

Many of our calls are for tankless flushing and error codes, not new installs. If you already own a unit that is acting up, ask us about repair before you replace it. We also serve nearby towns, including American Fork, and the full list is on the areas page.

Before You Buy

Questions To Settle Before You Buy

A 120 volt tankless unit draws far less power than a whole house model. That also means it heats a smaller flow of water. Think of it as a point of use heater for one sink or a small bathroom, not a replacement for a tank that feeds a shower and a dishwasher at once.

Check the flow rating on the box and compare it with the fixture. A bathroom faucet uses far less water than a shower, and a small unit can only raise the temperature so far when the incoming water is cold. Groundwater is colder in winter than in summer, so the same unit can feel weaker in January.

Ask about the outlet as well. A dedicated circuit and the right receptacle matter, and a licensed electrician should handle any new wiring. We can place the plumbing side, including shutoffs and any relief connection the maker calls for.
